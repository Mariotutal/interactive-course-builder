# Pedagogy: the teaching method

This method comes from a complete, validated course built with this approach. It describes **functions a lesson must perform**, not a template to fill in mechanically. Match each activity to what the outcome actually requires.

## The learning cycle

1. **Authentic start.** Open with a real problem, question, artifact or situation the learner could plausibly face, drawn from their stated goals and context. Avoid "In this lesson we will…".
2. **Intuitive model.** Offer an analogy or a simple model, and say where it **breaks down** (`limits`). An analogy without limits creates misconceptions that are hard to remove.
3. **Useful visual.** The visual should show a relationship, process, structure, evidence or choice that prose shows poorly. Always give a text equivalent. Decorative images do not count.
4. **Rigorous explanation.** Cover mechanisms, evidence, alternatives, limitations and when you would choose differently. Define each term before you rely on it, and link it to the glossary.
5. **Generate before reveal.** Ask the learner to predict, interpret, construct or decide *before* showing the answer. Wrong predictions are useful: give each wrong option feedback that names the misconception behind it.
6. **Worked example, then faded help.** Show one complete worked example. Some steps can be self-explanation steps (`selfExplain`). Then give guided practice with hints, then independent practice with no hints.
7. **Explanatory feedback.** Explain *why* an answer is right, and what reasoning leads to each wrong answer. Answer keys alone are not feedback.
8. **Check understanding three ways:**
   - **recall:** retrieval questions;
   - **application:** a new example;
   - **own words:** the learner explains the idea and compares their explanation with a model.
9. **Revisit and transfer.** End with a transfer task in a context **different** from the examples. Later lessons, spaced review and the capstone come back to earlier ideas.

## Depth without bloat

- Not every lesson needs every block. Use the `full` profile for lessons that introduce or develop an idea. Use `focused` for short review, drill, listening or reading lessons, which need an authentic start, an explanation, a check and sources. Across the course, every outcome still needs a transfer task.

- Depth means explanation, worked examples, realistic practice, misconception analysis, verification and transfer. Length is not depth, and many thin modules are not depth either.
- Aim for lessons that fit the learner's `sessionMinutes`. A typical lesson takes 25–45 minutes, with 1,500–4,000 words of learner-facing text excluding SVG. Split a lesson that runs longer, but do not cut its practice.
- Advanced material must be substantive, with the same functions as a core lesson. A reading list does not count as advanced material.

## Prerequisites and routes

- List each lesson's prerequisites explicitly, and make sure they come earlier in the course.
- When a prerequisite is outside the course, say where to learn it (`prereqText`).
- Always offer a **core route**, for a genuine beginner. Offer an **advanced route** only when depth or time allow substantive advanced lessons; a short course can have the core route alone.
- The optional diagnostic only changes the suggested pace, for example "quick pass: predictions + checks". It never hides content. Always offer a "start from zero" path.

## Evidence of learning

The runtime keeps four kinds of evidence apart, and so should your wording:

- **Visited:** the learner opened the lesson.
- **Attempted:** the learner interacted meaningfully with an activity.
- **Self-rated confidence:** the learner's own rating, 1–4.
- **Demonstrated:** a check question answered correctly on the first attempt.

Rubric checklists are self-assessed and are labelled that way. Never claim mastery from clicks or time spent. Where evaluation needs human judgment, such as pronunciation, essays or physical skills, say so and give criteria the learner or a tutor can apply.

## Varying the method by subject

These are examples, not a taxonomy:

- **Systems and engineering:** architecture explorers, parameter changes, fault diagnosis from evidence, constrained design, labelled real-world procedures with safety notes.
- **Mathematics and statistics:** manipulatives, worked derivations with self-explanation, distributions and sensitivity exploration, counterexamples, numeric checks with tolerance.
- **History and the humanities:** timelines with contested dates, source comparison (author, audience, purpose), competing interpretations, evidence-based argument assessed with a rubric (there is often no single correct answer).
- **Languages:** dialogues in context, comprehension, production with accepted variants, short frequent retrieval, and audio where supported. Speaking with people is real-world practice with external feedback.
- **Practical skills:** observation checklists, real practice, external feedback. The browser only structures the practice and records reflection.

Do not force technical labs, deterministic simulations, numeric scoring or a single correct answer onto subjects where they do not fit. Use `exemptions` and give the reason when a required function does not suit a lesson, for example a worked example in a pure listening lesson.

## Visual and interactive preference

A preference for visual and interactive learning is honored as a **design preference**, not a fixed learner category. Even with a "high" preference, every visual must explain something and every activity must require thinking. Even with a "low" preference, keep the visual and activity that carry the hardest idea, and always give text equivalents.
